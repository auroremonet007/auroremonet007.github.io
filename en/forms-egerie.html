Case study · EGERIE

Scaling an MVP for enterprise clients

How I improved the Forms module of a cyber risk management platform with a small team, by delivering first what unblocked clients.

Company
EGERIE, French publisher of cyber risk management software (Cyber GRC)
Role
Product Owner (work-study)
Period
Late 2025 – mid 2026
Team
1 front-end developer, 1 back-end developer, 1 QA engineer, supported by design and a Product Manager
Organization
Scaled agile (SAFe, 3 Program Increments), daily Kanban flow
Tools and methods
Jira, Notion · JTBD, job map, MoSCoW, Kano, impact/effort, Gherkin

Context

The Forms module lets companies collect information from their employees and suppliers, to assess the risks linked to these stakeholders. A risk manager creates a form, sends it to dozens of respondents, tracks the answers, then uses them in their analysis.

The module had been launched as an MVP. But enterprise clients were now using it at scale, sometimes for hundreds of forms a year. At the same time, the company was focusing its developers on its new platform. The challenge: improve the existing module with a very small team.

The problem

Friction was building up and support tickets were rising. By analyzing user journeys, I saw that creating, sending and filling in forms worked well. The breaking point was at the end, when users needed to work with the answers.

No exportable proof

To build their audit files, clients printed answers with Ctrl + P from their browser.

No alert on submission

Managers checked by hand, every day, whether forms had been completed.

Answers hard to reach

It took several clicks to find an answer and understand its status.

My role

I owned the topic end to end: needs analysis, solution design with the team, user stories, prioritization, workshops and follow-up through to release.

My approach

  1. 1

    Start from the field

    I analyzed client tickets, joined meetings where the topic came up, and gathered feedback from the sales and Customer Success teams. I also ran workshops with two pilot clients: an international industrial group and a large mutual insurance company.

  2. 2

    Turn requests into needs

    Clients came with ready-made solutions. By reframing them as Jobs-to-be-Done, I showed that “I want a PDF export” hid a deeper need: proving, during an audit, that a supplier had been assessed on a given date.

  3. 3

    Prioritize in three steps

    I put every request through a funnel:

    1. MoSCoW to set the scope;
    2. Kano to separate the essential from the nice-to-have;
    3. Impact/effort matrix, estimated with the developers, to spot quick wins.
MUST HAVEDelivered
  • PDF export of an answer
  • Direct access to statuses
  • Submission notification
SHOULD HAVEIf capacity allows
  • Show only changed measures
  • Adaptive criticality chart
COULD HAVEExcluded
  • Custom email templates
  • Calculated fields
WON'T HAVEPostponed to the new platform
  • Rules engine
  • Co-validation workflows
  • Open access without invitation
  1. 4

    Know when to say no

    Several requests affected the permission system or the data model. Building them on code due to be replaced would have been costly and created technical debt. I explained in writing to clients why these changes would not be made right away. They accepted a smaller scope, delivered faster.

  2. 5

    Specify for the team

    I wrote the user stories and their acceptance criteria in Gherkin, giving QA a clear testing framework.

What was delivered

Access

One-click access to answers, with clear statuses (In progress, Submitted, Expired).

Proof

A PDF export that matches the interface, with the date and the respondent's name, generated in the background without blocking the user.

Responsiveness

A notification sent to the manager as soon as a form is submitted, with a direct link to the answer.

Results

Fewer support tickets on the topics addressed
Export adopted on release, end of the Ctrl + P workaround
A client became a pilot partner to co-design the next version
No added technical debt: the export reuses the existing job queue

“A long-awaited feature.”

A large mutual insurance company, pilot client

What I learned

A client request is not a need: it must be broken down before building anything. Improving a product doesn't always require a redesign; a few well-chosen changes can unlock usage. And a transparent, well-argued “no” builds client trust instead of damaging it.
